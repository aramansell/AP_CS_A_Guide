Data Ethics & Privacy

CED topic 4.1 is not a programming topic. It is a judgment topic, and the exam tests it in the multiple-choice section through scenarios: a company collects X, what is the responsible use? You cannot answer those with code. You answer them with a vocabulary you build once and carry forever.

Anonymization Is Weaker Than It Sounds

Removing names is not a promise of privacy. Famous result: researchers re-identified people in "anonymized" datasets using as few as three data points (zip code, birth date, gender). The lesson for your work: anonymous is a technical state, not an ethical achievement. Re-identification risk grows with every extra column, and with every other dataset that exists in the world. player_id 101 is anonymous inside telemetry.csv. It stops being anonymous the moment some other file maps 101 to an account.

The Three Questions (memorize these)

  1. Could this hurt someone? Not "is it legal," not "did they click agree." Could a person be harmed if this data were used to its fullest?
  2. Would it survive the front page test? If tomorrow's headline described exactly what you did with the data, in plain words, would you defend it?
  3. Did the people know? Consent is real when it is informed, specific, and revocable. A 40-page policy nobody reads is legal cover, not consent.

The exam's responsibility questions are usually some arrangement of these three, dressed in a scenario about an app or a study or a company dashboard.

The Nudge / Dark Pattern Line

Helpful nudgeDark pattern

Both columns are the same technology. The difference is who the design serves. That is the entire ethics of engagement design in one sentence, and it is why "the code is the same" is never a defense.

Exam-Style Scenarios (practice)

Answer Like the Multiple-Choice Does

For each scenario, pick the MOST responsible answer, then defend it with one of the three questions:

  1. A fitness app wants to sell "anonymized" step counts to an insurer. The insurer also has customers' addresses and ages. Responsible? (Re-identification: zip + age + steps is a fingerprint. The answer is no, and the reason is the join.)
  2. A study tracks which students use the school's tutoring center and publishes "users of tutoring average 1 letter grade higher." Responsible? (Aggregation is fine; the risk is if the group is small enough that individuals are identifiable, or if usage data flows back to teachers unaggregated. The answer is "it depends," and the dependency is granularity.)
  3. A game offers a hard-mode badge that only 0.1% of players earn. The team wants to email everyone who did NOT earn it, "you are in the 99.9%." Responsible? (Motivational framing? Public shaming? The front page test answers this one instantly.)

Socratic Checkpoint

Why is a data ethics unit in a programming course?

Because programmers are the ones who build the collection. The tracking line of code does not look like an ethical decision, it looks like sessionMinutes++. Every privacy disaster in tech was written by someone having a normal day at the keyboard. The CED putting this at 4.1, before arrays even start, is a deliberate placement: the judgment comes before the power.

Does anonymization scale with size?

It gets WEAKER. In 30 rows, "Warrior level 12" might describe one person. In 30 million, it describes thousands. But larger datasets also enable more powerful re-identification joins. Size increases both anonymity and join power, and the join power grows faster. That asymmetry is why "we have so much data, nobody is identifiable" is usually backwards.
